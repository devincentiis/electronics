DIGITAL LINEAR POWER SUPPLY
=====================================

**PROJECT DESCRIPTION**

Electronics enthusiasts who want to try their hand at self-building a digital linear power supply for a small laboratory can create this device which in a single PCB brings together the essential functions to have a truly useful instrument.  
Taking inspiration from some circuit solutions of linear power supplies created in the past and available on the internet, this digitized model was conceived with microcontroller, DAC, LCD display and a single rotary encoder for interaction with the user.  
The objective is to be used with double winding 12-24 Vac transformers and a power of up to approximately 80VA capable of generating output voltages from 0 to 24 volts and currents from 0 to 3 amperes.  
With appropriate modifications the circuit could be scaled in both current and voltage. As it is it also accepts transformers with slightly different voltages, for example 15-30 or 10-20 Vac. At boot, the voltage supplied by the transformer is checked to set the maximum selectable via the rotary encoder and the possible presence of an intermediate socket.  
The power supply and the program also allow the use of single output transformers, at start-up the lack of the central socket is detected and indicated on the display and everything will continue to function regularly, however, it must be kept in mind that when we ask for supplies with high currents at low voltages, the two power transistors will have to dissipate a lot of heat and will force us to equip them with important heat sinks for cooling with forced ventilation.  
With a double winding transformer, however, the problem is halved as below the median voltage (also detected at switch-on) the relay will automatically switch to draw energy from the central tap of the transformer.  
The performance will never be the same as a modern switching power supply, but it will not be noisy. As you know, the operating principle is several decades old but is still considered valid in the laboratory where it is more likely to have problems with noise rather than heat.  
It can also be seen as a circuit to be connected to a transformer that has been in stock for many years and has never been used. In **Picture1** you can see one of the first creation placed into old media-center enclosure.

[<img alt="Picture" src="https://github.com/devincentiis/electronics/blob/main/DeVincentiisLinearPSU/picture1.jpg?raw=true" width="100%">](https://github.com/devincentiis/electronics/blob/main/DeVincentiisLinearPSU/picture1.jpg)

The firmware and wiring diagram are open source so they can be modified, resized, patched.  
An important improvement would be to replace the micro with an [STM32](http://www.st.com/en/microcontrollers-microprocessors/stm32f103ze.html) in the versions with two 12bit DACs, the considerable computing power would also [allow it to manage a touch display](https://github.com/ag88/Adafruit_ILI9341_SPI_stm32duino).  
Conversely, those who are used to Arduino Uno, Nano, etc. could replace the digital stage by adding another external DAC or replacing the current one with a two-channel one to control the analog stage.  
The presence of the I2C plug allows many expansions, for example by moving the LCD display onto it and equipping it with a PCF8574 module and using the [appropriate library for ATtiny](https://github.com/fdebrabander/Arduino-LiquidCrystal-I2C-library), thus leaving several pins free and if you have a transformer with more intermediate headers on the secondary it would be enough to modify the program to do so so that more steps of voltages can be chosen ( wiring diagram in **[Picture 3](https://github.com/devincentiis/electronics/blob/main/DeVincentiisLinearPSU/picture3.jpg)** ). If you want to do without the digital part while maintaining the switching of the transformer voltage, the circuit is simplified and you can base it on this: [**ANALOG SCHEME**](https://github.com/devincentiis/electronics/blob/main/DeVincentiisLinearPSU/analog_schematic.pdf)**.** Electronics maker often also have a 3D printer: they can have fun designing the front panel, the case and whatever else is necessary. The circuit uses the low voltage coming from the secondary of a transformer but any educational use on both the hardware and software side must be carried out under the **guidance of qualified and competent teachers who assume responsibility.**

**CIRCUIT DIAGRAM DESCRIPTION**

The alternating current coming from the double winding transformer 24-12 (or 30-15) enters J1, 0-24 Vac goes directly to the diode bridge D4 while the central one passes through D5 and rectify only the positive half-waves that arrive alternately from side 24 or from the 0 side; therefore when the relay is energized and therefore we are using half power the rectification using only half the diode bridge (- side) and the D5 diode, at full power it works in the classic way with only the full bridge. Regardless of the voltage chosen by the relay it will charge the smoothing capacitors C5, C7.  
D16 prevents that when the relay switches from 12 to 24 the charge present on C26 flows onto the smoothing capacitors with a high current, tending to "stick" the relay contacts, especially if they are of poor quality.  
The branch that passes through R1, C1, C2, C3, D1, D2, D3 is used to obtain a negative voltage of 3.1V for the operational amplifiers U2, U5, U7 in order to allow them to bring the output to 0V compared to PWRGND; it is an old and well-known circuit solution which has also been stabilized with an [LM337](https://www.ti.com/product/LM337L). Here the fictitious resistor is not needed to always keep the diode bridge under load in order to generate voltage on the negative branch as the function takes care of the absorption due to the power supply of the integrated circuits. The 24 volts are obtained with a [BD139](https://www.st.com/en/power-transistors/bd139.html) (Q2) coupled to the zeners and which supports input voltages up to 80V compared to the 40V of a possible 7824, the R42 resistor prevents the peak current at start-up from exceeding the maximum collector of the transistor , therefore the 24V line serves the operational amplifiers, the relay, the fan (60mA or more if we cool Q2 well) then continues towards the regulator for the 5V of the digital circuits U6, U8, display. Due to the fact that when the device is turned off the relay would vibrate dangerously because both the leveling capacitors C5-C7 and C17 remain charged, and therefore the microcontroller U6 served by it, it was necessary Q10 an NPN BJT which goes into conduction only during shutdown, it has the same behavior as Q8 (which we will see later) but it is used to communicate to the microcontroller not to turn the relay back on to use HALF\_PWR even if the output voltage is below half as the shutdown is in progress.  
The digital stage is based on U6, a suitably programmed [ATtiny3216](https://www.microchip.com/en-us/product/attiny3216) microcontroller; it is the heart of the entire system and represents the distinctive feature of this linear power supply. Let's analyze it starting from the analog inputs and outputs to and from the control operational amplifiers.  
The voltage is set by the user with the EC11 rotary encoder and comes out from PIN 1 (V\_SET line) of U8, a 12 bit [MCP4725](https://www.microchip.com/en-us/product/MCP4725) DAC, it is to this that the micro communicates the value to be provided at the output via I2C. The V\_SET line is filtered by the low-pass R22, C21, R20, C16 from any very small noises and goes to the non-inverting input of U7 which with the transistors Q5, Q4, Q6 and Q7 raises the voltage based on the gain set by calibrating trimmer RV3 which with R28 and R21 constitutes the feedback on the inverting input. A\_SET is the line coming out of the single internal DAC of the [ATtiny3216](https://www.microchip.com/en-us/product/attiny3216), it has a resolution of only 8 bits; consequently there will be 256 steps, it will be ensured that with each increment of the rotary encoder the current will vary by 20mA so as to allow the current limiter to reach even 5.1A. Since the reference voltage inside the micro of 4.3V is used, the increase for each step corresponds to 16.9 mV which through R14 ends up on the non-inverting input of the op-amp U5 used as comparator between this and the voltage coming out of U2 which instead it goes to the inverting input. When the current limiter is activated due to a current at the limit, the pin6 output of U5 is brought downwards and through diode D12 forcing the voltage to a lower one than that coming from V\_SET. The operation amplifier U2 together with the resistor R12 constitutes the current amplification and detection stage and serves both for the aforementioned current comparator/limiter U5 and for the micro to measure the current that the power supply is actually supplying at that moment in order to indicate it on the display. Let's delve deeper into its operation which is not very intuitive due to the fact that R12 and U2 are far apart in the electrical diagram. Just observe that the non-inverting input is connected to the negative output -Vout while the inverting one upstream of R12 or on the PWRGND line, as the current increases the PWRGND line will become negative with respect to -Vout which is the mass reference of the our microcontroller, but as you can see U2 is in an inverting configuration so its output will be rising when the input is falling, i.e. when the current increases. With R9 and R10+RV1 the gain is established and calibrated as we will see later . It should be noted that due to the fact that the micro and the external DAC supply the reference voltages to the analogue stages they have been placed downstream of R12, i.e. on the -Vout output; consequently they represent a bias absorption of a few mA (about 20) which however would be shown on the display if we did not compensate them via RV6 (Current offset). Another analog value to read is evidently that of the voltage present on the output, it is obtained sending it on the V\_READ line after partitioning it with R32 and R33. It will be used to display it on the display, and if we use a dual transformer, the relay will be activated when the voltage drops below that measured on the central tap, with the double benefit of increasing efficiency and decreasing the heat generated by the final transistors . When switched on, the voltages supplied by the transformer are checked. In parallel to the leveling capacitors C5, C7 you can observe another resistive divider R6, R7 which, like what has already been seen for the output, is sent on the DETECT\_TRANSFO\_VOLTAGES line; when the device is started the firmware is designed to check the presence of the central socket and read the voltage, but the wait to be able to do this would be very long if the Q9 transistor controlled by the microswitch via the CAP\_DISCHARGE line which provides for the discharge of the smoothing capacitors after activating the relay, the voltage drops to the median in the case of a dual transformer (Picture 2), or goes to zero in the case of a single output transformer.

The transistor Q8 placed on the base of Q5 of the final stage is always disabled and does not intervene during normal operation since on the cathode of the zener D15 we will have -0.1V (3.0-3.1) and will never reach the threshold of 0.7V compared to the 'emitter to be able to go into conduction; however, it becomes indispensable at the moment of switching off when all the (considerable) charge accumulated on C5 and C7 would cause the output voltage to rise in an uncontrolled manner. When you turn off the power supply, the -3.1V line will be floating almost immediately and in any case long before the +5V leveling capacitor of the digital stage is discharged. Since the negative 3.1V on the anode of the zener D15 is immediately missing, the voltage on the base of Q8 rises, bringing it into saturation, interdicting Q5, Q4 and finally the final pair Q6, Q7 as well as communicating it to the microcontroller via Q10.Transmitting this information to the micro offers the further possibility (from version 20240609 of the firmware) of remembering the current and voltage settings by writing to the EEPROM only when the power supply is turned off and not at every change, so as to significantly extend its life which is limited and guaranteed for 100,000 writes.  
The capacitor C18  perform integration and the function of avoiding self-oscillations when current limiter, to check if work fine you can connect an oscilloscope between TP1 and -Vout.
The final stage and the entire analogue part originated by observing mainly the diagrams that are public on the internet, in particular in a circuit sold by the Chinese the three op-amps are powered directly by the voltage of the leveling capacitors, this greatly limits the possible scalability of the voltage unless using particular and expensive op-amps that work at higher voltages, in fact many on the network have had to replace the [TL081](https://www.st.com/en/amplifiers-and-comparators/tl081.html)s with TLE2141s; instead this scheme powers them at around 27V and then amplifies it as we will analyze later. The first operational U2 is in inverting configuration as a compensated amplifier and is used both by the micro to measure the current and to be compared on U5 with the one chosen by the user (A\_SET).
As already mentioned, the final stage was also created by observing what others had already done in the past, but not uncritically, some DIYers following the scheme of a kit published in 1992 capable of delivering 8A (10A peak) did not notice that the same suffers from non-linearity of the pilot transistor (Q5) which on the original had too steep a conduction angle. To correct this defect, resistor R49 was added and the polarization resistors R23-R24 changed. 

**PRACTICAL IMPLEMENTATION**

To assemble this power supply you will need to obtain all the components in the list given above and the PCB shown in the renderings of **Picture 4** and **Picture 5**. You could create the latter as you wish by routing the electrical diagram with an EDA software or obtain it via the contacts you will find at the end of the article.  
It is preferable to solder the components by hand first on the surface mounted ones, respecting the order: resistors, capacitors, diodes, transistors, integrated circuits. Those who have a soldering plate or even a reflow oven could also choose to use resistors with smaller packages than the 1206 indicated in the parts list. To facilitate the assembly of the through-hole components, it is preferable to start from the lowest and finish with the highest, in our case the two leveling electrolytics.  
The two [TIP3055](https://www.st.com/en/power-transistors/tip3055.html) transistors can be mounted directly on a nearby heatsink and connected with wires of appropriate cross-section. There are aluminum profiles known as tunnel profiles on the market, they are very efficient if equipped with a fan that you can power directly from the appropriate plug on the PCB.  
The two LEDs D10 (green) and D11 (red) respectively indicate the activation of the relay for selecting the transformer output and the achievement of the limit current. They must be mounted on the front panel with the rotary encoder, the LCD display and the output sockets. You will choose the positions best suited to the aesthetics, tastes and geometry of the container you have decided to use.

**FIRMWARE UPLOAD**

As mentioned in the presentation, the firmware you find in this document is free, therefore open source, and was written for the [**IDE Arduino**](https://www.arduino.cc/en/software) to which some libraries found on github.com have been added. In particular, [**megaTinyCore**](https://github.com/SpenceKonde/megaTinyCore) software support was used to program the [ATtiny3216](https://www.microchip.com/en-us/product/attiny3216). It allows you to program all the microcontrollers of the tinyAVR series 0,1 and 2 family. It was chosen from series 1 because it has an internal DAC exposed on pin4 (PA6), the one already discussed in the wiring diagram and is used to set the current limiter, and 6 because it has a sufficient number of I/Os for the purpose. These modern devices can be easily programmed via a single pin, 16 in this case, hence the name UPDI.It is very convenient to use an Arduino Nano as a programmer on which the [**jtag2updi**](https://github.com/ElTangas/jtag2updi) firmware has been loaded and connected via a 4.7 KΩ resistor on the PD6. If you have never done it, the web will help you, you will find many guides.

The code is fairly commented and therefore easy to read.  
Only small parts are written in pure AVR-GCC, for the rest Arduino libraries are used to simplify it and make it more intuitive.  
As many of you know, the void setup() function is the one that is executed only once at startup and therefore provides for the typization of the I/O pins, the initialization of the Wire, LCD and Rotary libraries; if your IDE does not have them you must install them by going to Tools → Library Manager → Filter your search e.g.: “SimpleRotary”  
The first operation that is performed is to energize the relay and discharge the leveling capacitors to measure the presence of the central tap of the transformer. After waiting 2 seconds and turning off the transistor Q9, we wait another few moments to measure the voltage which, if greater than a few Volt indicates the presence of central tap and also the value of the middle\_voltage variable.  
The max\_voltage variable is read after having de-energized the relay, the protection is activated if it is detected as too low (< 18V) or too high, blocking the execution of the program after having energized the relay (if too high), bringing the output to 0V and indicating the problem on the display.  
If everything falls within the parameters established on the firmware, the display will indicate the type of transformer and its voltage(s) for 2 seconds. Finally, the previous voltage and current values are read on the EPROM to reset them on the two DACs.  
Considering that the **[sketch]** seen above uses only 25% of program memory, there is still a lot of space left to add other functions and a more complex menu.

**CALIBRATION**

When the device is turned on for the first time, the contrast trimmer of the RV4 LCD display must be calibrated, otherwise we will not be able to display any messages; normally to have a more or less good vision the voltage value on the center of this potentiometer is around 1V. If you have programmed the [ATtiny3216](https://www.microchip.com/en-us/product/attiny3216) correctly you will see the messages otherwise a line will be displayed with sixteen full rectangles on the first line and nothing on the second. If it is the first time that the microcontroller is used and we have not set the power value of the transformer we are using on the EPROM, it will ask us to do so and through the rotary encoder we will choose the value in VA that is closest to ours, otherwise this operation it will no longer be requested and to do it again at a later time you will have to keep the encoder button pressed immediately after switching on the power supply and until the transformer VA setting is proposed again. Once the program has booted, two lines will appear on the display, the first is Out: where the output voltage and current are indicated. The second line is Set: which displays the set threshold voltage and current. Every time you press the button on the rotary encoder you go from setting the output voltage to the limit current or vice versa. The second trimmer to adjust is RV3 (V gain), this is done with the rotary encoder by bringing the voltage to the maximum possible and the limit current to 0.50A and without load. RV3 will then be rotated until the output voltage value (Out:) coincides with the one set (Set:); lower the voltage with the rotary encoder to Set: 0.1V and adjust RV5 (V offset) until reading 0.1V also on the output voltage. If necessary, RV3 is touched up again after bringing the tension back to the maximum possible. The third adjustment concerns the calibration of the current measurement, for this reason we act on RV1, A gain and RV6 A offset. If you do not have an electronic load you will have to use a tester in series with a load that absorbs a current of approximately 1A, after having rotated the encoder to the maximum current allowed by the transformer, adjust RV1 until it reads Out: it same current value marked by the tester; then by decreasing the voltage to almost zero we will ensure that the current is 20 or 40 mA i.e. "0.02A" and we will go around RV6 to adjust the offset (zero) of the current, similar to what was done above for the tension. Finally, RV2 will be adjusted, this is used to ensure that the current limiter begins to intervene on exactly the same value requested and displayed on the second line of the display (Set:). Therefore, assuming that the load used for calibration absorbs, for example, 1.52A, Set: will also be brought to 1.52A, therefore RV2 will have to be rotated to the position in which the limiter does not intervene but is about to do so, turning on the red LED D11, for confirmation of the exact position by turning the econder one click anti-clockwise i.e. 1.50A the LED will have to turn on, returning it to 1.52 it will have to turn off.
